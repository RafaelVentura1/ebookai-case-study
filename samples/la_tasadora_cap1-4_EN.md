# The Appraiser — Chapters 1–4

> **About this translation.** The novel was written in Spanish. This English version was translated with AI assistance (Claude, by Anthropic); the original Spanish text is shown, unedited, in `la_tasadora_cap1-4_ES.md`.
> Four small slips in the original were corrected in the translation only: a grammar slip (not visible in English); the color of the metal under the hairpin's enamel (light gray in chapter 1, copper in chapter 4 — now light gray in both); a photocopy described with the relief of a typewriter (now toner); and a scene break added before the archive scene in chapter 4. Sabin's switch from the formal *usted* to the familiar *tú*, which Spanish shows on its own, is made explicit in a few words.

## The Hairpin in Box Seven

The door at the end has two locks at different heights: the upper one with a knob; the lower one a latch, its bolt sticking out. Both are open. The wood is dark at the bottom, where hands go looking for it, and along the top edge runs a lighter strip.

The first thing I notice is the shut-in smell. The doorman gave me the key with the visitors' book open on the counter and his finger pointing to the line where I was to sign in, but in here the air has that dry heat of dust left to sit still, because the apartment has had its windows closed since September.

I go in and the floor creaks under my feet; it isn't the creak of old wood. I stand in the middle of the room and wait for the apartment to go quiet, and it takes its time. Against the back wall there is a row of boxes.

I count them from where I stand, my finger in the air. I get twenty-three. I count them again by columns, because there's no room for assumptions in an inventory; I get the same. Corrugated cardboard, all the same size, stacked against the wall with the label side facing out. Each label is a rectangle of card tied with string to the handle. The handwriting is small, in blue and black ballpoint; the date at the top, written out in full, and below it the box number in capitals.

I take out my phone and photograph the label on box 1, but the flash bounces off the cardboard; I correct the angle and shoot again. Then I go on to the next ones. When I reach the fifth, the string has given way and the label spins on itself; I hold it with the edge of my left hand, without touching it with my fingers, and shoot. The sixth is crooked; I straighten it. The seventh is stuck on with tape instead of string, and the edge of the card has lifted a little.

I open the seventh and the lid gives with the sound of cardboard coming unstuck. Inside, on yellowed tissue paper, there is a hairpin.

It's black metal, with two waves, the tip on the right-hand side chipped; underneath, the light gray metal shows through. I pick it up between my thumb and forefinger and turn it; it weighs what it should weigh, and the chipped tip scratches the pad of my thumb a little. I recognize it before I know I recognize it. It isn't a hairpin like mine: it is mine. The one I kept in the toiletry bag in the bathroom when I was little, the one that kept falling out of my hair, and my mother would tell me not to lose it, that hairpins get lost and then turn up in the strangest places. I hold it between my fingers and my thumb goes looking for the tip, checks it, presses it a little into the skin until it leaves a mark.

I rest my free hand on the floor, which is cold; the hand stays there, open, while my thumb keeps at the hairpin. I don't put it back in the box. I put it in the inside pocket of my coat, against the lining, where it won't get mixed up with anything, because I don't want it confused with the material of the job. Not in my bag, where the hardback notebook and the papers go.

I carry on with the rest. Two boxes along, the labels have been written twice, the first crossed out with a line. Another is dated 1998, another 2001, another 1996. The dates aren't in order, like things put away in a hurry and sorted afterwards, or by some logic other than the calendar's. So they don't follow a chronological archive.

Between two boxes at the back there is one more, behind and half hidden; its lid is slightly open and a hand's width of tissue paper sticks out. The label says 1994. The date is handwritten, in the same small hand, but the box number isn't there: there's a blank space where it should be.

I lift the lid with two fingers: on the tissue paper there is a key.

Two teeth, darkened brass, with a cardboard tag tied through the bow with a thin cord. The tag is smaller than the ones on the boxes and has the date written on it by hand, in the same blue and black ink. I pick it up. The brass is cold and slow to warm; I turn it over and I recognize it by the wear on the bit, by the notch in the second tooth, by the edge polished from turning so many times in the same lock. It's a key I recognize: the one I always believed belonged to a house I knew, the one that hung behind a door on a nail, with a strip of black electrical tape so it wouldn't slip.

I put it with the hairpin, in the same pocket against the lining, because I don't want each find rattling around loose. My hand is slow to come out; when it does, it's closed.

Box 1 is at the start of the row, the first on the left. I go over to it. The label carries a date I read twice: 1991, and the number in capitals: BOX 1. I look at the date and then at the cardboard. 1991. I know perfectly well what year that is. I know it the way I know the dates on the deeds I appraise and the registry numbers I copy into reports. It's a year that comes before my date of birth, and that means nothing, because a house can hold things from before you were born, and a dead man's boxes can carry any date at all.

I don't open box 1. I photograph the label; the flash bounces again, the photo comes out blurred and I take it again. I move on to the next ones and keep going until I've done all twenty-three that have numbers. When I finish, I open the hardback notebook and copy the dates and numbers onto the inventory sheet, one by one, with the pen from my bag. My handwriting comes out smaller than usual.

My phone rings. I look at the screen: Begoña. The name flashes up with the photo I took of her in the kitchen, apron on, hand raised in a half-wave. I hesitate; the call keeps ringing, and I remember that I haven't told anyone I'm here. No one. Not even her.

I swipe.

"Yes?"

"Amaia. Have you finished?"

Her voice reaches me with the kitchen behind it: the faucet, the stream of water, something moving in a pot. She's cooking or doing the dishes, probably both.

"Almost."

"And how is it? Lots of junk?"

"Boxes."

"Boxes." The faucet shuts off with a bang. "And what's inside?"

"Papers, mostly. Clothes. Household things."

"Now then, sweetheart." Her voice drops a tone, turns more practical. "The door at the end has two locks, did you know? You have to turn them in order. The top one first."

I look at the door. It's open; the top lock with its greenish brass. Both open, the way I found them.

"It's already open."

"What?"

"It's already open. The door." I move the phone away from my mouth for a moment, just long enough for my breathing not to show. "The doorman opened it, I suppose. Or it was like that."

"Ah." A short silence, with the sound of a spoon against the rim of a pot. "Well. All the better. And the cardboard? Is the cardboard all right? Those apartments are very damp, and cardboard soaks up the damp and then there's no lifting it."

"It's fine."

"And the labels? Can you read them?"

"You can read them."

"Well." The spoon again. "That's that, then, sweetheart. When you're done, call me and I'll tell you how we'll do it. And don't forget to check the radiator in the living room, those ones give off a lot of heat and then you know what happens."

"Okay."

"Now then, sweetheart, and where does this go?" she says, and for a moment I don't know what she means, until I hear a cupboard door and understand that she's talking to the laundry, or the groceries, or whatever is in front of her. "Nothing, never mind me. I'll let you go. Call me."

"Okay."

She hangs up first. I stand with the phone in my hand, looking at the dark screen, and then I look at the door again. The top one. Turn them in order, the top one first.

I haven't told her I opened the boxes, or that inside the seventh there was a hairpin, or anything about the key, because I don't want her asking about them before I know what they mean.

I put the phone in the pocket of my bag, leave the room, cross the hallway past the kitchen without looking, and reach the elevator. The metal door closes with a dull thud; inside, the light is yellow and the mirror has a chipped corner. I press my arm against my side and feel the hairpin and the key against the lining: two hard lumps, one beside the other, because I want to make sure they're still there.

The elevator goes down; the floors pass with a hum. It stops at the third, the door opens, there's no one, it closes again.

In the lobby, next to the intercom, I take out my phone: there's a message from Begoña, sent a minute ago, "inventory?"

I stare at the word and the question mark, stuck together with no space, as if typed in a hurry. Behind the glass of the door, the street has its first-thing-in-the-morning color, the light still low and cars double-parked. A man goes by pulling a shopping cart and looks into the pharmacy window.

I put the phone away and go out, because there's nothing to answer to that word. The key to the street door, the one the doorman gave me, weighs in my coat pocket, beside the two-toothed one; the two-toothed one is no longer where it was: it's in my pocket and I haven't recorded it.

I walk to the corner without turning my head.

## Not on Record

I come in off the street with the case file in my bag and a doubt that won't let me take the stairs in one go: whether what's in the back room is appraised as movable property or counted as part of the property itself and left out. So I climb slowly, the blue folder poking out of my bag whenever I put my hand on the stone banister. Before I open a single box, I need to know whether to count it or describe it as floor space.

In the meeting room, beside the metal filing cabinets, the oval table smells of polished wood, and case file SU-1174/25 is already open on the far side, its pages squared to the edge. Mercedes Arrieta doesn't look up. Her glasses hang from her neck and one hand rests on the sheet. The fluorescent light hums with the persistence of an old refrigerator.

"Good morning," I say.

"Sit down." She points to the chair opposite with her index finger, without looking at me. The wall clock says ten past nine, its tick swallowing the hum now and then.

I take out the hardback notebook and the pen. Before I've fully sat down, I already have the question in my mouth. I ask it in the voice I use for figures.

"The contents of the back room. Do they go into the inventory of assets, or are they considered part of the property?"

Mercedes turns a page. The nail of her index finger travels up to the top margin, where there's a note, and stays there. Then she lifts her hand slightly and leaves it hanging. She writes nothing.

"Whatever isn't inventoried doesn't exist for the auction," she says. "It's that simple."

My pen stops over the blank page. I repeat it to myself. The sentence doesn't relieve me: it gives me somewhere to put myself, like a drawer with a lock. If something isn't on record, there's nothing to argue about. But not being on record doesn't erase it; it just leaves it on the outside, with me.

"And what has to happen for it to be on record?" I ask.

"Appraise it, date it, and enter it." At last she looks up. Her glasses gleam under the fluorescent light. "If something can't be appraised, you describe it and record its condition. What you can't do is keep quiet about it and hope nobody asks."

She turns the folder around and slides it toward me. A printed email is stapled to the sheet, short, with a postscript asking how many boxes there are. It's signed Fernando Aranguren. That means the heir is keeping his own list, and every box I fail to count will be a box he counts as missing.

"The heir wants the date in writing," says Mercedes. "And he wants to know how many boxes."

"How many are there?"

"As many as you count." Her tone doesn't change. "He's keeping count on his side."

I hear the click of a pen. Unai Mendieta is standing by the metal shelving at the back, passing a pen from one hand to the other at a slow rhythm, like someone keeping a beat nobody asked him for. He hasn't said anything. When he sees me looking at him, he repeats the last word he heard, even though he heard it from me.

"Boxes."

Mercedes doesn't look up.

"If the inventory gets complicated," she says, "Unai is available."

The pen passes from one hand to the other. The click, again. I rest my elbow on the edge of the table and take it away at once. The metal of the filing cabinet was cold.

"How many heirs are there?" I ask.

Mercedes covers the pen with her hand and sets it down on the sheet.

"Enough that nobody wants a scandal."

I write the date in the notebook. My handwriting comes out small, pressed against the margin. I underline the reference number, SU-1174/25, twice: the second time harder than the first, because the first wasn't enough.

"The auction date?" I say.

"It's on the notice." Mercedes points to a line with her finger. "It's on record there."

I look at the line. The date is printed, and someone has gone over it in red marker. Below it, in the margin, there's a penciled note that isn't in the same hand: *Confirm inventory before the 12th*. I don't ask who wrote it. If I asked, I'd have to explain why a penciled note unsettles me more than the date in red, and I have no explanation I could put in a report.

"That's that," says Mercedes, and takes her finger away.

I don't ask about the hairpin, or the key I'm carrying in my coat pocket against the lining, and I don't mention the boxes I've already seen or the one I haven't opened. I already know what I'm going to do: the boxes in the back room will be described one by one, with their date and number, and what I keep in my pocket won't appear on any sheet. Mercedes's sentence gives me solid ground. If what isn't inventoried doesn't exist, then my pocket is the only place where those two pieces can go on being mine without anyone asking.

"Anything else?" Mercedes asks.

"No." I close the notebook. "Nothing else."

On my way out, the assistant is waiting for me by the reception counter with a copy of the schedule stapled to a sheet of instructions. Her nails are short, and she holds the paper out to me with both hands, the way you hand over something heavy.

"Your copy."

I take it. The staple grazes the tip of my thumb, and the paper smells of hot toner. On the landing, before going down, I look at the sheet: the auction date has been gone over in red, and below it, in pencil, the same small handwriting I've already seen in the file. I run my fingertip over the first letter of my own name, printed in the header, and feel the faint ridge of the still-warm toner. Then I fold the sheet in half and put it in my bag, next to the notebook. I go down the stone stairs without turning my head.

## Family Visit

The smell of machine coffee reaches me before the chill of the lobby does: a stale reek stuck to the wall. It's there every afternoon. The machine is nowhere to be seen.

Behind the counter, Sabin raises his head from the newspaper and lowers it again. With that gesture he has already seen me. I wait for him to decide how long to let pass before saying hello.

I rest the notebook on the wood, by the worn edge, and wait for him to fold the page. The page folds with a crackle, like a bandage coming unstuck. I speak.

"I need a copy of the service key. For the visits I have left."

Sabin reaches into his breast pocket, takes out a handkerchief and removes his glasses. He wipes them slowly, one lens and then the other. The air in the doorman's booth smells more of coffee and less of damp. The door stays shut. He puts the glasses back on and settles the arm with his index finger.

"Have a seat for a moment, if you like."

"I'd rather stand."

He nods. He was expecting that. He opens the visitors' book at the last page written on and runs his finger down the margin, top to bottom, until he finds a line.

"Nobody comes in here without being written down." His voice comes out slow, unhurried. "And the service key isn't copied without the notary's authorization. It's not me saying so. It's them."

"The notary already knows I'm working here."

"That I have on record." He points to a line in the book with his fingernail. "But it's on record a different way."

I look where he's pointing. The line says, in Sabin's handwriting, broad and a little crooked: *4A — family visit — 14:30*. Next to it, a scrawl. The date, three days before the appraisal.

I put my finger on the paper, on the date. I read it once. I read it again. I ask.

"Who was it?"

"An elderly lady." Sabin shrugs, and the shrug looks too small on him. "She said she was family of the deceased. I wrote down what she told me."

"Which side of the family?"

"She didn't tell me that." He closes the book with one hand, without fuss. "Here we write down who comes in and what for. The rest is none of my business."

I take my phone out of my bag, open the camera and photograph the page. The flash bounces off the green of the covers and the string that ties the pen, like a coin striking the bottom of a well. The click sounds louder than it should. The lobby is narrow. Sabin doesn't look away. He puts the handkerchief back in his pocket and waits for me to finish.

I turn to the bulletin board. Between the notice of the residents' meeting and the insurance receipt, a sheet has come loose at one corner and someone has pinned it back with a different thumbtack. The receipt is dated last month, and the stamp has run where it got wet. I don't touch it.

"The neighbors are worried about the façade," Sabin says behind me. "That the auction will damage it. You know how it goes: first the scaffolding, then the sign, then the noise."

"The façade isn't part of the lot."

"That's what I tell them." There's the rustle of his work coat against the stool. "But me saying it isn't the same as reading it on paper."

I put the phone back in my bag. I don't tell him the file has the deceased down as a man with no descendants. I think it and keep it to myself; the silence leaves a metallic taste in my mouth, like biting on a label by mistake.

"And the key?" I ask.

Sabin moves the handkerchief from one pocket to the other without using it.

"Tomorrow or the day after, when I've talked to the notary, I'll leave it for you on the same hook." He points with his chin at a brass hook to the right of the counter, empty. "You'll find it here."

"Tomorrow or the day after."

"As soon as they get back to me." And he adds, his voice lower still: "It's not that I don't want to give it to you."

It's the first time he has dropped the formal *usted* with me. I notice it and say nothing. I pick up the notebook from the counter, open it at the last page written on and note, in handwriting pressed against the margin: *service key — pending — hook at counter*. I underline the word hook.

"Thank you, Sabin."

"Thank you."

As I go out, the air in the street hits me in the face, cold and greasy as a used kitchen rag. It smells of diesel and of fresh bread from the bakery on the corner. I look at the hook from the door, before it closes. Still empty. I'll have to come back.

## The Six-Centimeter Ruler

The hairpin measures six centimeters, and that's the only thing I can say about it for certain.

I lay it on the blank white sheet, in the center, and set the metal ruler beside it. The metal of the ruler is cold; so is the hairpin's, but differently, as if it had kept the cold of the box instead of the room's. I nudge the zero with my thumb until the tip of the left-hand wave lines up with the mark. Six exactly. The chipped tip falls a millimeter short, and I note that millimeter too.

I turn the hairpin under the lamp. The black enamel has flaked off in two more places I hadn't seen this afternoon: a small one on the curve, another that goes down to the metal and leaves a glint of light gray. The white light of the lamp flattens the shadows and makes everything look cleaner than it is. My thumb finds the notch of the chip and runs along it. By the third pass the pad has warmed and I can no longer tell the relief of the metal from the relief of my own skin.

I pick up the phone, place the ruler next to the hairpin, shoot. The screen is slow to focus. When it finally does, I see the photo the way a buyer would: metal object, six centimeters, wear at the tip, no visible mark. Estimated value: none. Use value: none. Description for the report: accurate.

I open the notebook at the last page written on. My handwriting comes out small, pressed against the margin, as if the paper charged by the square centimeter.

*Black metal. Two waves. Enamel chipped at the tip. Box 7. Date 1993.*

I lift the pen and hold it still for a moment over the word date. I don't underline it. I close the notebook and take my wallet out of my bag.

My ID card is where it always is, behind the bank card. I lay it next to the photo of the box 7 label, which I've had printed on a sheet since this afternoon. The date of birth on the card sits on the right; the date on the label, on the left. Between them, the white surface of the table. I look at one. I look at the other. I look at the distance between them.

All my life I've known I was born after that date. It's a fact that admits no argument. And yet I sit looking at the two figures the way you look at a picture hung crooked.

The hum of the refrigerator rises suddenly, as if someone had opened its door and shut it again. I don't turn around. Across the courtyard, a television says something I can't make out and a woman laughs over the top of the sentence. The lamp hums too, higher, and the sheet has been left with one corner raised that moves every time I breathe.

I take an envelope from the drawer, put the hairpin inside, and close it without wetting the flap. The envelope bulges in the middle, the shape of the object pressed into the paper. I take it to my coat, which hangs on the back of the chair, and slide it into the inside pocket until it touches the lining. The key was already there. The two pieces meet and make one sound, a small click, and then the coat goes still again.

I don't call my mother.

I sit down. The seat of the chair is cold and my back can't find a position. I put my hands on the table, one on each side of the sheet, and look at the photo of the label without fully seeing it.

There's one thing I learned before anything else: to count the kitchen drawers. I'd have been six or seven. I'd sit on the wooden stool and open the cutlery drawer, close it, open it again. "One," she'd say. "Two," she'd say, and I'd repeat it.

Then I'd fold the napkins in half, and in half again, and put them on the pile on the left, which was the good ones, or on the pile on the right, which was the everyday ones. If I got it wrong, nobody scolded me. She'd open the drawer and start again. "Now then, sweetheart, and where does this go?" And I'd put it in its place, and she'd shut the drawer with her knee, and the dry thud meant it was right.

I learned to put things in order before I learned to read. I learned that things have a place and that the place can be checked. That if you count twice and get the same, there's no mistake. That what isn't in the drawer hasn't been lost: it's somewhere else, and you have to look for it. My mother never told me there was anything to look for.

My thumb finds the notch on the ruler again, the six-centimeter one, and I run along it until the mark grows warm. Then I pick up the ruler and put it in the drawer, and the click of the catch sounds louder than it should in an apartment where there's only me.

In the newspaper archive's reading room the air is so dry that the first breath scrapes. Outside it's raining. The drops hit the windows in gusts, and between gusts you can hear the silence of the reading machines, all switched off, like a row of empty refrigerators.

The request form lies on the counter, its top edge slightly curled. I fill in the boxes in my usual handwriting. Full name. ID number. Reason: research into a property. The clerk waits standing, hands folded on the counter, not watching my hands.

"Date of birth too," she says.

I write it. The same as always. The one on the card. The pen stops for an instant over the signature, its tip resting on the paper, and the ink begins to spread into a blue dot that grows slowly. I lift the pen. The dot stays there, round, darker at the center.

"Are there auction notices in the local press?" I ask. "From the nineties."

"Yes." The clerk leans forward a little. "The 1994 holdings are in storage. You have to request them in advance."

"How far in advance?"

"A couple of days. And they have to be read here in the room." She points with her chin at the switched-off microfilm readers. "You look at them there."

I look at the machines. The black screens give back a reflection of the room: shelves, my coat, the rain on the glass. The 1994 holdings. A couple of days.

"Thank you," I say.

I fold the form in half, careful not to crease the signature, and put it in my bag. I don't hand it in. The clerk says nothing; she has already gone back to her own work, sorting some index cards that haven't moved.

I sit at the table nearest the window. The rain slides down the glass and behind it there's nothing, only the gray of the street. I open the notebook at the last page written on and note, in handwriting pressed against the margin: *1994 holdings — storage — request in advance*. And under it I add the word *request*, and cross it out, and write it again, and leave it.

I close the notebook. The microfilm readers are still switched off, in a row, and the hum in the room is really the heating, rising and falling without anyone touching it.
